## Module 1: Cybersecurity Fundamentals

* **Module Number:** 1
* **Module Title:** Cybersecurity Fundamentals
* **Why This Matters:** Cybersecurity is not only the responsibility of an IT or security team. Everyday actions—opening an email, choosing a password, sharing a file, connecting a device, or clicking a link—can either reduce risk or create an opportunity for an attacker.
* **Major Sections:**
  1. **What Is Cybersecurity?:** Definition (protecting devices, accounts, networks, systems, applications, and information from unauthorized access, misuse, damage, theft, or disruption). Real-World Analogy: Physical Office vs. Digital World (Door lock $\leftrightarrow$ Password, ID card $\leftrightarrow$ MFA, Security guard $\leftrightarrow$ Security team, Security camera $\leftrightarrow$ Security monitoring, Restricted room $\leftrightarrow$ Protected system/data).
  2. **Why Is Cybersecurity Important?:** Modern reliance on technology (Email, Cloud, Mobile, Computers, Banking apps, messaging, websites, databases, business apps, online services). Consequences of attacks: Loss of information, financial loss, privacy violations, service disruption, reputation damage, identity theft, loss of customer trust. Example: University database containing student records (unauthorized access affects confidentiality; secretly changing grades affects integrity; database unavailable during exams affects availability).
  3. **The CIA Triad:** Confidentiality (Keep it secret/private), Integrity (Keep it correct), Availability (Keep it accessible). These form the main goals of info security.
  4. **Understanding the Cyber Threat Landscape:** Definition of threat. Categories: Cybercriminals (financial gain, steal credentials, commit fraud, deploy ransomware, take over accounts), Insider Threats (legitimate access but creates risk, accidental or malicious), Nation-State Threats (espionage, intelligence, disruption, access sensitive govt or industrial info), Opportunistic Attackers (search for easy opportunities like weak passwords, unpatched software, exposed systems). Analogy: checking neighborhood doors to find unlocked ones.
  5. **Common Cyberattacks:** Malware (Viruses, Trojans, Spyware, Ransomware) and Phishing (pretends to be bank, manager, IT support, delivery, university, trusted online service).
  6. **Social Engineering:** Manipulating human decision-making rather than technical weaknesses. Attackers exploit Fear, Urgency, Authority, Curiosity, Trust, and Impersonation.
  7. **Common Social Engineering Techniques:** Phishing, Smishing (SMS text), Vishing (voice calls), Pretexting (false story), Impersonation, Baiting (offering something attractive).
  8. **Password and Account Security:** Good password habits (unique passwords, avoid predictability, do not share/reuse, use password managers, enable MFA). Danger of reuse: credential stuffing.
  9. **Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA):** Adds another step. Three common factors: Something you know (password/PIN), Something you have (phone/key/authenticator), Something you are (biometrics).
  10. **Human Role in Cybersecurity:** Users must protect credentials, verify requests, handle info carefully, report suspicious activity, update devices, follow policies, and use approved software.
  11. **Real-World Scenario: The Fake Invoice:** A finance employee receives an email requesting payment to a new bank account. Best action: Independently verify the request using known contact information and organizational procedures.
  12. **Tool Spotlight: Have I Been Pwned?:** Public service to check if an email appeared in known breaches. Indicates need to review security, change exposed passwords, use unique passwords, and enable MFA.
* **Interactive Exercises:**

## Module 2: Password & Account Security

* **Module Number:** 2
* **Module Title:** Password & Account Security
* **Why This Matters:** Your online accounts are often the gateway to personal information, academic records, communications, files, and other digital resources. An attacker does not always need to break into a complicated system; guessing a password is often enough.
* **Major Sections:**
  1. **What Is a Password?:** Secret used to authenticate and prove authorization. Examples of accounts: email, university, social media, cloud, banking, gaming, work, developer.
  2. **Strong vs. Weak Passwords:** Weak is easy to guess, predict, or obtain (based on name, birthday, phone, university, favorite team, simple sequences, common words, repeated characters, e.g., password123, 12345678, qwerty123, Ali2007). Strong is difficult to guess and unique.
  3. **Password Length Matters:** Length is major part of password strength (short password "Tiger7" vs. longer passphrase "river-lamp-coffee-window").
  4. **Password Complexity:** Uppercase, lowercase, numbers, symbols, less predictable combinations. Changing "password" to "Password1!" is still predictable. Better Principle: Length + Uniqueness + Unpredictability.
  5. **Password Reuse:** Using the same password on multiple accounts is dangerous; leads to credential stuffing. One compromised account becomes multiple compromised accounts.
  6. **Common Password Mistakes:** Using personal info, common passwords, reusing passwords, sharing passwords, writing in unsafe locations, using predictable patterns (e.g., Password1, Password2, Password3).
  7. **Password Managers:** Securely store and manage passwords, generate unique passwords, retrieve when needed, reduce reuse. You must protect the master password manager with strong protection and MFA.
  8. **Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA):** Adds layer beyond password. Factors: Something you know (password/PIN), Something you have (phone/key/authenticator), Something you are (biometrics).
  9. **Why MFA Matters:** If an attacker gets your password, they are still blocked by the additional verification step. Never share codes with anyone.
  10. **Protecting Your MFA:** Be cautious when asked for OTPs, recovery codes, security keys, or login approvals. Unexpected request = Stop and verify.
  11. **Account Takeover:** Unauthorized person gains control of someone's account via stolen passwords, phishing, credential stuffing, malware, social engineering. Attackers attempt to: change settings/passwords, access private info/files, send fraudulent messages, lock out legitimate user.
  12. **Warning Signs of Account Takeover:** Alerts, reset emails, unexpected MFA notifications, messages sent that you did not write, settings changes, unknown devices, password stops working.
  13. **Credential Theft:** Attacker obtains credentials without authorization via phishing, fake logins, social engineering, malware, reuse attacks, fake support messages.
  14. **Fake Login Pages:** Visually imitate email, portal, bank, cloud, social media. Check: address, domain, expected login, redirects, spelling, urgency, source. Don't trust professional appearance alone.
  15. **Password Reset Scams:** Fake messages like "A password reset has been requested... Click immediately to cancel." Warning signs: unexpected, urgency, unexpected domain, asking for password/MFA code. Safer approach: do not use link, go direct, use official reset process.
  16. **Protecting Important Accounts:** High-priority: primary email (master key to resets), university, financial, cloud, work, password manager, developer.
  17. **Account Security Checklist:** Granular questions (Do I use unique password? Sufficiently long? MFA enabled? Checked login activity? Recovery current? Recognize devices? Know how to report/recover?).
  18. **Interactive Tool — Password Strength Checker:** Purpose is to teach factors (length, repeated patterns, common patterns, predictability, overall strength [Weak, Moderate, Strong, Very Strong]).
  19. **Privacy Rule for Password Checker:** Do not permanently store passwords. Ideally evaluate locally or process temporarily. Fictional test password requirement: Use fictional password (e.g., "Training-Example-Only-2026!"), never real ones.
* **Interactive Exercises:**
  * **Scenario (MFA Prompt):** Someone calls claiming IT department: "I need the code that just appeared on your phone." (Correct Action: C. Do not provide code and independently verify).
  * **Interactive Challenge — Build a Better Account:** University account creation. Safest approach? (Correct Action: C. Create unique password and enable MFA).
  * **Interactive Challenge — Spot the Scam:** Spotting signs in university account deletion scam. Warning signs identified: Urgency, Request for password, Request for MFA, Threat of account closure, Pressure to act immediately. (Correct Action: All of the above).
  * **Scenario — Unexpected Login:** Alert "New login detected from unfamiliar device." Action? (Correct Action: C. Investigate through official account security page and secure).
* **Scenarios (Final Challenge — Secure the Account):**
  * *Scenario 1:* Create unique password and enable MFA. (Answer: SAFE).
  * *Scenario 2:* Message asks you to send password so support can verify. (Answer: SUSPICIOUS).

  * *Scenario 4:* Use same password for email, university, and social media. (Answer: SUSPICIOUS).
  * *Scenario 5:* Use fictional password in Password Strength Checker. (Answer: SAFE).

  * Strong account security starts with long, unique, unpredictable passwords.

## Module 3: Phishing & Social Engineering

* **Module Number:** 3
* **Module Title:** Phishing & Social Engineering
* **Why This Matters:** Attackers do not always need to break through sophisticated technical defenses. Sometimes, they try to manipulate people into making the security mistake for them.
* **Major Sections:**
  1. **What Is Phishing?:** Social engineering technique where attacker tricks a person into revealing info or performing unsafe action. Pretends to be: bank, university, manager, IT support, delivery, govt, social media, colleague. Habit: Unexpected + Urgent + Sensitive = STOP AND VERIFY.
  2. **How Phishing Works:** Step 1: Create believable story (account locked, payment failed, package undelivered, password expiring); Step 2: Create pressure (act immediately, deleted today, only 10 minutes); Step 3: Request action (click link, open attachment, enter credentials, send info, approve login, pay); Step 4: Capture result (credentials/info/money).
  3. **Email Phishing:** Uses email to deceive. Warning signs: unexpected, urgent language, requests sensitive info, suspicious sender, suspicious links, unexpected attachments, grammar issues, threats, bypass normal procedures. Risk: High.
  4. **Smishing:** SMS/text phishing. Banks, delivery, universities, operators, govt, stores. "Your parcel could not be delivered... Confirm address." Warning signs: unexpected, unknown numbers, links, urgency, requests payment/personal info. Safe Response: do not trust link; open official application/website independently.
  5. **Vishing:** Voice/phone phishing. IT support, bank, university, customer service, manager, technical support. Attacker attempts to obtain authentication secret: "Give me verification code immediately." Correct Response: do not provide, end call, contact organization through known trusted channel.
  6. **Social Engineering:** Manipulation of human decisions. Exploit: Fear, Urgency, Authority, Trust, Curiosity, Greed, Helpfulness. Formula: Manipulation $\rightarrow$ Emotion $\rightarrow$ Action. Attackers want emotional reaction before verification.
  7. **Urgency Tactics:** Urgency rushed feel leads to: skipping verification, ignoring signs, clicking immediately, revealing info. Habit: Urgency is a reason to slow down, not a reason to hurry.
  8. **Fear Tactics:** Frightening recipient ("compromised", "permanently closed", "identity flagged"). Emotional trigger. Ask yourself: "What happens if I do nothing for five minutes and verify this independently?"
  9. **Authority Impersonation:** Pretending to be manager, teacher, IT staff, banker, govt official. Authority does not automatically mean legitimacy. Verify through trusted channel.
  10. **Fake Login Pages:** Visual imitation of email, portal, banking, cloud, social media. Check address, domain, expected request, how reached, normal process. Habit: Never trust login page simply because it looks real.
  11. **Suspicious Links:** Destination hiding. Before clicking check: who sent, expected, destination, domain match, spelling, urgency. Example: visible text says "University Student Portal" but links to unrelated domain. Rule: Check destination, not just displayed text.
  12. **Suspicious Attachments:** Unexpected documents, spreadsheets, archives, executables, fake invoices, notices. Warning signs: unexpected, unknown sender, urgency, unusual actions, identity uncertain. Safe Response: do not open simply to "see what is inside"; use reporting process.
  13. **Requests for Sensitive Information:** Passwords, MFA, bank, personal, recovery, confidential, identity. Rule: Legitimate need does not automatically make unexpected request legitimate. Verify independently.
  14. **The Human Manipulation Cycle:** 1. Trigger emotion (Fear/Urgency/Curiosity/Trust) $\rightarrow$ 2. Reduce thinking time $\rightarrow$ 3. Request action (Click/Open/Login/Send/Approve) $\rightarrow$ 4. Gain result (Credentials/Info/Access/Money). Habit: Pause before the action.
  15. **How to Verify Suspicious Communication (STOP Method):**
    * **S — Stop:** Do not click, reply, open, approve, or provide info immediately.
    * **T — Think:** Ask (Was I expecting? Make sense? Rushing me?).
    * **O — Observe:** Check (Sender, Domain, Link destination, Attachments, Wording, Request).
    * **P — Prove:** Verify through independent trusted channel (contact manager via known phone number, not reply to email).
  16. **Interactive Tool — Phishing & Message Checker:** pasted email, SMS, message analysis. Privacy rule: do not paste real passwords, MFA codes, banking, or sensitive personal info. Use fictional/sanitized examples.
  17. **Checker Engine Analysis:** Urgent language, Sensitive requests, Suspicious wording, Impersonation indicators, Suspicious links, Social engineering techniques.
  18. **Risk Assessment Levels:**
    * **LOW RISK:** Few indicators. Recommendation: continue normal awareness. Example: normal informational message.
    * **MEDIUM RISK:** Some indicators suspicious. Recommendation: do not use link, verify via official service. Example: unexpected account activity review.
    * **HIGH RISK:** Multiple strong indicators. Recommendation: do not interact, verify independently and report. Example: unexpected message with urgency, password request, MFA code request, suspicious link, threat of closure.
  19. **Phishing Response Decision Tree:** EXPECTED? (No $\rightarrow$ Cautious) $\rightarrow$ SENSITIVE INFO? (Yes $\rightarrow$ Stop and verify) $\rightarrow$ URGENCY/THREATS? (Yes $\rightarrow$ Suspicious) $\rightarrow$ LINK/ATTACHMENT? (Yes $\rightarrow$ Verify before interacting) $\rightarrow$ INDEPENDENTLY VERIFY SENDER? (Yes $\rightarrow$ Verify through trusted channel, No $\rightarrow$ Do not interact and report).

## Module 4: Safe Browsing & Device Hygiene

* **Module Number:** 4
* **Module Title:** Safe Browsing & Device Hygiene
* **Why This Matters:** Your browser and devices are constantly connected to the digital world. Everyday activities (visiting websites, downloading, installing, opening documents, Wi-Fi, logging in, notifications, updating software) can introduce security risks. Attackers use fake websites, unsafe downloads, malicious apps, outdated software, or deceptive ads to compromise devices.
* **Major Sections:**
  1. **What Is Safe Browsing?:** Using internet in a way that reduces chance of being tricked, infected, or exposed. Checking website addresses, avoiding suspicious links, downloading from trusted sources, keeping browsers updated, avoiding pop-ups, being careful with sensitive info, using secure account practices. Rule: Don't trust website simply because it looks professional.
  2. **Website Awareness:** Consider: Who owns? Is it recognized? Why visiting (navigated there or sent there)? What info requesting (passwords, MFA, bank, personal, identity)? Does URL make sense?
  3. **Understanding HTTPS:** Protected using encryption during transmission. *Important Limitation:* HTTPS does not automatically mean website is trustworthy; malicious websites can use HTTPS. HTTPS = encrypted connection, not guaranteed legitimacy. Example: `https://fake-example-site.com` has encrypted connection but is untrustworthy.
  4. **Checking the Domain Name:** Domain name is most useful to inspect. Legitimate `university-example.edu` vs. attacker `university-example-login.com` (different domain!). Look for: unexpected words, misspellings, extra words, unusual endings, unrelated domains, look-alike names.
  5. **Look-Alike Websites:** Designed to resemble legitimate services by copying logos, colors, fonts, login forms, layouts, branding. Habit: Look beyond design; check the actual domain.
  6. **Suspicious URL Structures:** Extremely long strings, unnecessary subdomains, random characters, unexpected words, misleading organization names, unusual paths. Indicators requiring context.
  7. **IP-Address-Based URLs:** Direct IP access (e.g., `https://203.0.113.10/login`). IP-address-based URL is not automatically malicious but unexpected login directing there deserves caution. Principle: An unusual URL is an indicator—not proof. Consider context.
  8. **Excessive or Misleading Subdomains:** Complicated structures like `official-login.company.example-attacker.com` where actual registered domain is `example-attacker.com`, not `company.com` or `trusted-company.com`. Rule: Identify the actual domain instead of relying on familiar words anywhere in the URL.
  9. **Suspicious Keywords in URLs:** `verify`, `secure`, `login`, `account`, `update`, `confirm`, `security`. Not inherently malicious, but warning indicators when combined.
  10. **Browser Security:** Keep browser updated, install extensions carefully, remove unused extensions, avoid unknown extensions, notification permissions caution, limit information access, avoid saving sensitive info on shared devices, review settings.
  11. **Browser Notifications:** Deceptive websites abuse "Allow notifications?" to display unwanted or deceptive messages. Safer Practice: only allow when recognize, understand why, and actually want. Otherwise, Block.
  12. **Suspicious Pop-Ups:** Urgent/fear messages ("infected!", "won a prize!", "expired!", "Click here!"). Website pop-up should not be trusted as genuine device-security notification. Safer Response: Close page/tab, use legitimate local security tools to check.
  13. **Unsafe Downloads:** Risky sources (unknown websites, unofficial repos, suspicious ads, email attachments, untrusted file sharing, pirated software). Danger: malware, unwanted software, spyware, malicious installer, modified software. Safer Practice: Use official/trusted sources.
  14. **Fake Software Downloads:** Advertising legitimate software (e.g., "Official Browser Update — Download Now") but delivering malware. Signs: unexpected download, unfamiliar site, not official source, triggered by pop-up, extreme urgency, unusual file name. Rule: Do not install software simply because a website tells you that you need it.
  15. **Software Updates:** Important part of security. Provide: security fixes, bug fixes, stability, compatibility, new features. Update regularly: web browsers, OS, applications, security software, device firmware.

## Module 5: Data Handling & Privacy at Work

* **Module Number:** 5
* **Module Title:** Data Handling & Privacy at Work
* **Why This Matters:** Information is one of the most valuable assets in an organization. Employees work with customer data, student records, financial files, business plans, internal communications, passwords, contracts. A security incident does not require hacking; accidentally sending right info to wrong person is a serious incident.
* **Major Sections:**
  1. **What Is Data?:** Information stored, processed, communicated, used (names, emails, records, reports, images, system info). Organizations depend on it. Protecting data is critical.
  2. **What Is Sensitive Information?:** Information that could cause harm, privacy problems, financial loss, or security risks if exposed, changed, or misused.
  3. **Personal Data:** Information relating to an identifiable person (name, email, phone, address, student ID, employee info, account, associated info). Spreadsheet example: Ali, Ahmed with IDs/emails contains personal info. Cannot share publicly.
  4. **Confidential Business Information:** Not publicly disclosed (strategies, financial reports, contracts, customer lists, product plans, internal investigations, unreleased projects, security docs, credentials). Sharing product design publicly before auth can harm.
  5. **Personal vs. Organizational Information:** Categorizing data (Personal data $\leftrightarrow$ Employee phone; Customer data $\leftrightarrow$ Customer contact; Financial data $\leftrightarrow$ Payment records; Confidential business data $\leftrightarrow$ Business strategy; Technical data $\leftrightarrow$ Internal system docs; Public info $\leftrightarrow$ Published company website). Handling depends on classification.
  6. **Data Classification:** organizing according to sensitivity and protection:
    * **Public:** Generally shared publicly (website content, announcements, job ads).
    * **Internal:** For use within organization (procedures, communications, schedules).
    * **Confidential:** Accessible to authorized (customer records, financial docs, business plans, employee records).
    * **Highly Confidential / Restricted:** Requires strong access controls, unauthorized disclosure causes significant harm (sensitive personal info, business secrets, credentials, investigation info).
  7. **Why Data Classification Matters:** Answers "How should I handle this?" Determines: who can access, where stored, how shared, encryption, transmission, retention, disposal.
  8. **The Right Person Is Not Always the Right Recipient:** Wrong recipient mistake (e.g., sending customer report to `accounting@example.net` instead of `accounts@example.com`). Rule: Verify the recipient before sending sensitive information.
  9. **Email Data-Sharing Mistakes:** Check recipient, CC/BCC, attachment, content, permission, destination before sending.
  10. **Accidental Disclosure:** Unauthorized disclosure occurs when info shared with unauthorized person. Can happen accidentally (sending wrong person, attaching wrong doc, posting publicly, sharing private cloud link incorrectly, group email include confidential, uploading to unauthorized platform). Accidental does not mean harmless.
  11. **Data Leakage:** Information becomes accessible to unauthorized via mistakes, misconfigured cloud storage, weak access, lost devices, unsafe sharing, unauthorized apps, compromised accounts. Folder set to "Anyone with link" containing confidential docs is leak.
  12. **Cloud Storage Awareness:** Platforms, drives, collaboration, documents. Before sharing ask: who can access? link public? restricted? recipient need? platform approved? classified? expire later?
  13. **Public Links vs. Restricted Links:** Option A (Anyone with link) vs. Option B (Only authorized). Give minimum access necessary (Least Privilege).
  14. **Privacy:** how personal info collected, used, stored, shared, protected. Ask: need this? who needs access? why collecting? where stored? who can access? sharing more than necessary?
  15. **Data Minimization:** Avoiding unnecessary collection, storage, sharing of personal info (less stored = less exposed).
  16. **Need-to-Know Principle:** Sensitive info available only to people who need for legitimate purpose. Access based on business need, not curiosity (finance employee needs access to financial records, unrelated department does not).
  17. **Unauthorized Disclosure:** Friend asking to see confidential employee document "just to see" what contains. Friends promise not to share is irrelevant. Correct Response: do not share.
  18. **Clean Desk & Screen Awareness:** Lock screen when stepping away. Avoid leaving sensitive info on unlocked computer, desk, whiteboard, printer, shared screen.
  19. **Removable Storage:** USB drives risks (loss of files, malware, copying, accidental disclosure). Use approved storage, do not connect unknown devices.
  20. **Data Sharing Decision Process:** STOP (pause before sending) $\rightarrow$ CHECK (info & recipient) $\rightarrow$ VERIFY (auth & communication method) $\rightarrow$ SHARE (only what necessary) $\rightarrow$ CONFIRM (went to intended destination).

## Module 7: Security Incident Recognition & Reporting

* **Module Number:** 7
* **Module Title:** Security Incident Recognition & Reporting
* **Why This Matters:** Not every cybersecurity problem requires a technical expert. A normal employee, student, or user may be the first person to notice something unusual. Recognizing warning signs early and reporting correctly helps respond before situations escalate.
* **Major Sections:**
  1. **What Is a Security Incident?:** Event that may threaten the CIA of information, systems, devices, or accounts. Examples: unexpected logins, lost devices, accidental data exposure, malware alerts, unexpected password changes, suspicious files, unauthorized access.
  2. **Security Incident vs. Normal Event:** Distinguishing normal actions (system-requested password changes) from potential incidents (unsolicited password change notifications). Rule: Unexpected + Security-related = Stop and consider reporting.
  3. **The Five-Step Incident Process:** 
    1. **RECOGNIZE:** Notice something unusual.
    2. **STOP:** Avoid making things worse (stop clicking, stop opening, stop entering info, stop sharing).
    3. **PRESERVE INFORMATION:** Keep evidence available (time, date, screenshots, errors, files).
    4. **REPORT:** Contact approved channel (security team, help desk, supervisor, email).
    5. **FOLLOW INSTRUCTIONS:** Allow responsible team to guide next steps.
    * *Rule:* Your role is to recognize and report—not perform advanced forensics.
  4. **Step 1 — RECOGNIZE Details:** Warning signs (login alerts, password changes you didn't make, unknown devices, missing devices, malware warnings, suspicious files, unusual activity, accidental exposure, access indicators). Only need enough info to recognize.
  5. **Step 2 — STOP Details:** Do not attempt random fixes without guidance. Example: warning displayed, do not keep opening files to "see what happens." Stop. Preserve. Report.
  6. **Step 3 — PRESERVE INFORMATION Details:** Preserve: Time, Date, screenshots, errors, sender, email subject, device, location, file name, account, observations. Do not alter/delete. Don't immediately delete a suspicious message if reporting rules require preservation.
  7. **Step 4 — REPORT Details:** Use approved channels. Rule: When in doubt about a potential security incident, report it through the appropriate channel.
  8. **Step 5 — FOLLOW INSTRUCTIONS Details:** Do not become your own investigator. Follow security directions (change password, disconnect device, stop account use, preserve device, run scan, wait). Rule: Follow instructions from the responsible team.
  9. **Suspicious Login Activity:** Unexpected "New sign-in detected" alert. Indicators: unknown location, device, browser, unexpected time, multiple failed attempts. Do not ignore, check legitimacy, preserve, report.
  10. **Possible Account Compromise:** indicators (alerts, password stops working, password changed without action, unexpected MFA prompts, sent messages you didn't write, settings changes, unknown sessions, recovery activity). Unexpected account activity deserves attention.
  11. **Unexpected MFA Prompts:** Spamming requests. Means someone is trying to access. Action: do not approve unexpected requests, don't share codes, preserve, report. Rule: Never approve an authentication request you did not initiate.
  12. **Unexpected Password Changes:** Notification "Your password was successfully changed." Warning sign. Process: Recognize $\rightarrow$ Stop $\rightarrow$ Preserve $\rightarrow$ Report $\rightarrow$ Follow instructions.
  13. **Lost or Stolen Devices:** Incident risk due to stored emails, documents, sessions, credentials. Action: report promptly; do not wait hoping it returns.
  14. **Accidental Data Exposure:** Human mistakes (wrong email, public sharing cloud, public posting). Do not hide the mistake. Stop $\rightarrow$ Preserve $\rightarrow$ Report.
  15. **Malware Warnings:** Software alerts ("Malware detected"). Actions: stop interaction, note warning, preserve, report, follow instructions. Do not: keep opening, disable security, delete info, install random programs, attempt analysis. Rule: The goal is safe reporting, not amateur forensic investigation.
  16. **Suspicious Files:** Unexpected, unknown sender, named strangely, associated with unusual messages. Do not open because sender says "Open immediately."
  17. **Unauthorized Access Indicators:** sessions, notifications, devices, changes, file modifications, sent messages, resets, MFA requests. Justifies reporting.
  18. **When Should You Report?:** Report account, device, data, and technical problems. Rule: If you reasonably believe something may have affected security, report it.
  19. **What Info to Include in Report:** Factual details (WHAT happened? WHEN? WHERE? WHO? HOW? WHAT DID YOU DO?).
  20. **Good vs. Poor Incident Report:** Poor ("Something weird happened") vs. Better ("At approx 3:15 PM I received two MFA requests..."). Report facts, not guesses.
  21. **Interactive Tool — Incident Report Assistant:** Practice creating clear, specific reports. Asks: what, when, which device/account, what observed, what actions taken. Suggests improvements to user inputs.
  22. **Incident Severity Awareness:** Lower concern (suspicious event, no exposure), Greater concern (potential unauthorized access), Serious concern (potential exposure of sensitive info/loss of device). User must report, let security team determine final severity.
  23. **Incident Response: What Not to Do:** Investigating beyond role, destroying information, hiding mistakes, guessing, continuing activity, taking unauthorized actions.
  24. **Incident Response Golden Rule:** Don't make the situation worse while trying to fix it. First responsibility: Recognize $\rightarrow$ Stop $\rightarrow$ Preserve $\rightarrow$ Report $\rightarrow$ Follow Instructions.

## Module 8: Email & Business Communication Security

* **Module Number:** 8
* **Module Title:** Email & Business Communication Security
* **Why This Matters:** Email is one of the most important communication tools in organizations. Used to communicate with colleagues, exchange docs, receive invoices, discuss projects, customers, share info, request payments. Because widely used, attackers target it to steal credentials, deliver malware, redirect payments, obtain info, impersonate executives.
* **Major Sections:**
  1. **Business Email Security:** Accounts contain high-value info. Compromise exposes info and allows employee impersonation. Rule: Treat business email as a business system—not just a messaging app.
  2. **What Makes a Business Email Suspicious?:** Warning signs (unexpected requests, urgent language, suspicious attachments, suspicious links, unusual sender, requests for confidential info, requests to change payment details, unexpected invoices, executive impersonation, requests bypassing normal procedures). Multiple indicators increase caution.
  3. **Suspicious Attachments:** Risks of malware. Be cautious if unexpected, unknown sender, inconsistent with conversation, associated with urgent, requests enabling unusual functionality, triggers security warnings. Example: "URGENT — PAYMENT DOCUMENT" attachment. Stop $\rightarrow$ Verify $\rightarrow$ Report.
  4. **Suspicious Links:** Direct to fake logins, malicious sites, credential stealing, unexpected downloads, impersonation. Ask: who sent, expected, destination, make sense, verify before clicking.
  5. **Urgency Is a Warning Sign:** Urgency forces acting without thinking ("Pay immediately", "closed today", "CEO needs in 10 minutes", "Do not contact anyone"). Rule: Urgency should increase verification—not reduce it.
  6. **Fake Invoices:** Appear like legitimate business payment requests. Company branding, invoice numbers, payment instructions, bank details, professional formatting. Appearance does not prove legitimacy.
  7. **Payment or Bank-Detail Change Requests:** Critical business email risk ("Please use new bank account", "Supplier has changed payment details"). Rule: Do not rely only on email. Verify changes through independent trusted communication (existing contact info, verified phone number, approved internal procedure). Never trust requests just because they come from a familiar-looking address.
  8. **Business Email Compromise (BEC):** Fraud where attackers use email or compromised accounts to deceive. Goals: fraudulent payments, theft of sensitive info, compromise, changes, impersonation. Example: attacker impersonates executive, requests urgent payment.
  9. **Executive Impersonation:** Pretend to be CEO, manager, director, finance, customer, supplier. Example: From CEO, Subject: URGENT PAYMENT. CEO card purchase scam: "I'm in a meeting. Purchase gift cards immediately... don't call me." Signs: urgency, unusual request, bypass communication, financial impact, impersonation. Correct Action: do not purchase, verify and report.
  10. **Sender Verification:** Never rely only on display name (e.g., Muhammad Ali — Finance Department can have totally different actual email). Check full address, domain, previous communication, context, expectation. Rule: A familiar display name does not prove email is from that person.
  11. **Look-Alike Domains:** Slightly altered legitimate domains (`company-example.com` vs. look-alike `company-examp1e.com` or `trusted-companny.com` with double 'n'). Pay attention to spelling, extra characters, replaced letters, domain endings, additional words.
  12. **CC and BCC Security:** CC (Carbon Copy) visible to all; avoid copying people into sensitive conversations. Rule: Include only people who have a legitimate reason to receive the information. BCC (Blind Carbon Copy) hides recipients; useful when recipients shouldn't see each other's addresses (announcements). *Constraint:* BCC is not a replacement for proper access control. Do not use to hide inappropriate sharing.
  13. **Misaddressed Emails:** Typing mistake sends info wrong person (`ahmad@company.com` vs. `ahmad@another-company.com`). Check To, CC, BCC, attachments, content before sending.
  14. **The "Pause Before Send" Rule:** BEFORE clicking send: STOP (don't immediately send) $\rightarrow$ CHECK (recipient, CC, BCC, attachment, info, links) $\rightarrow$ SEND (only after confirming).
  15. **Sensitive Information by Email:** Ask: approved method? recipient need? address correct? attachment correct? additional protection required? Rule: Convenience should not override security requirements.
  16. **Interactive Tool — Email Security Checker:** Concept to help identify suspicious characteristics. Inputs: sender address, subject, email body, links, attachment name. Analyzes sender, message indicators, financial requests, links, attachments.
  17. **Email Security Checker Example:** Input: `ceo-office@company-support.example`, Subject: URGENT — Need Payment Now, Body: "I'm currently unavailable...". Flags: urgent language, financial request, executive impersonation, bypass verification, unusual sender domain. Risk Level: HIGH RISK. Recommendation: Do not make payment, verify, report.
